Chord Progressions in Music Theory

A chord progression is a sequence of chords played in order, forming the harmonic backbone of a piece of music. Progressions give a song its sense of movement, tension, and resolution, and they're one of the main tools that make a piece of music feel "familiar" even the first time you hear it.

Roman Numeral Notation

Chords are often labeled with Roman numerals representing their position, or scale degree, within a key, rather than a fixed note name. This lets the same progression be described no matter what key it's played in. Uppercase numerals (I, IV, V) are major chords, and lowercase numerals (ii, iii, vi) are minor chords.

Common Progressions

I–IV–V — the foundation of countless blues, rock, and folk songs.

C – F – G  (in the key of C major)

I–V–vi–IV — often called the "pop progression," heard across decades of popular music.

C – G – Am – F

ii–V–I — the essential cadence of jazz harmony, creating strong forward motion toward the tonic.

Dm – G – C

I–vi–IV–V — the "50s progression," common in doo-wop and early rock and roll.

C – Am – F – G

Tension and Resolution

Progressions work because chords have different relationships to the tonic (the "home" chord, I). The V chord (the dominant) creates tension that naturally wants to resolve back to I. This V→I motion is called a cadence, and it's the harmonic equivalent of a sentence reaching its period. Progressions that end on V (a "half cadence") feel unfinished, while those ending on I (an "authentic cadence") feel complete.

Why It Matters

Understanding chord progressions helps you predict where a piece of music is headed, write your own songs, improvise over changes, and recognize the shared harmonic DNA between songs that otherwise sound nothing alike.
